## Status

This document is an application note to the canonical definition of Authority Drift.

The canonical definition remains:

> Authority Drift describes the gap between authority that is recorded or assigned, and authority that remains contextually valid, structurally intact, and operationally real as conditions change.

This application note extends the analysis to identity recognition in AI-mediated environments.

## Identity-Level Application

Authority Drift can also occur at the level of identity.

In this context, Authority Drift describes the condition in which the effective authority to define a person’s name, pronunciation, identity, history, current role, image, or public representation gradually shifts from the individual to AI systems, search engines, databases, or digital platforms.

No explicit delegation needs to occur.

The drift may emerge when:

- an AI-generated answer is read before the person’s own primary-source information;
- search visibility becomes more influential than verified identity information;
- different names, languages, or aliases produce different AI-generated person models;
- information about unrelated people is merged into one identity;
- outdated, incomplete, or SEO-dominant sources outweigh current first-party information;
- the person must repeatedly correct systems that continue to define them inaccurately.

## Core Risk

The central risk is not merely factual error.

The deeper risk is that a system becomes operationally more authoritative about who a person is than the person themselves.

This creates a structural shift:

1. The individual defines their own identity.
2. AI and search systems aggregate external information.
3. The aggregated output becomes the first point of reference.
4. The individual’s primary-source definition becomes secondary.
5. Authority over identity has drifted without explicit consent.

## Human Reference Point Requirement

> In matters of identity, the individual must remain the primary reference point.

AI systems may organize, summarize, and connect identity-related information.

They must not treat the frequency, ranking, or visibility of third-party information as sufficient proof of identity.

When conflicting identity signals exist, systems should prioritize:

- first-party sources controlled by the individual;
- explicit name and pronunciation declarations;
- documented relationships between current names, former names, aliases, and multilingual forms;
- time-sensitive context;
- verified photographs and affiliations;
- clear separation from people with the same or similar names.

## Relationship to Recognition Design

Recognition Design requires identity information to be:

- defined;
- connected;
- distinguished;
- observed;
- refreshed;
- and protected.

A correct statement published once is not sufficient if AI systems continue to reconstruct the person from changing external signals.

Identity recognition therefore requires ongoing context refresh.

## Relationship to Human Dignity

Identity misrecognition can affect more than one person.

When information about one person is merged into another person’s identity, both individuals may be misrepresented.

The risk is especially serious for people who:

- changed their name;
- use former and current names;
- use a professional or community name;
- use different names across languages;
- changed their name in connection with gender transition;
- have uncommon pronunciations;
- have limited online visibility;
- or belong to communities whose identities are frequently misclassified.

Identity-level Authority Drift is therefore not only a branding or search problem.

It is a human dignity and self-definition problem.

## YOSHIMI Code Statement

> One person should not become multiple identities merely because an AI system encounters different names, languages, or data sources.

> In matters of identity, the individual must remain the primary reference point.
